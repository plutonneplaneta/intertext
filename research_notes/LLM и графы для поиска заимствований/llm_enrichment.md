# Local 7B-class LLMs as an additional signal / re-ranker for biblical allusion detection in Russian literary text

## What concrete roles can a 7B-class instruction-tuned LLM play (judge, NLI, perplexity, zero-shot, synthetic data)?

### Takeaway
The literature supports LLM-as-judge/verifier and LLM-based synthetic-data-generation as the most mature, implementable roles; conditional-perplexity/surprisal scoring is technically well-understood (from membership-inference research) but has not been directly validated as a borrowing-detection signal in published work; zero-shot cold-search identification of allusions by an LLM over a large reference corpus is explicitly reported as not scalable; NLI/entailment scoring is a well-established technique family that has not yet been applied to this specific literary-allusion task but transfers naturally from paraphrase detection.

### Cited Findings
- Umphrey, Roberts & Roberts (arXiv:2409.01882, Sept 2024) feed documents into an LLM which outputs detected intertextual pairs (direct quotations, allusions, echoes) in natural language, subsequently validated by domain experts (biblical Koine Greek corpus); the authors state this pipeline "generates novel intertextual observations" but explicitly note it "is not suitable for large-scale applications and is confined to case-specific studies," and that the LLM has difficulty with long query passages and introduces false/spurious textual dependencies requiring expert oversight — [arXiv:2409.01882](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.01882)
- A 2025 survey of quantitative intertextuality methods in the digital humanities (arXiv:2510.27045) categorizes LLM use into three nascent patterns: (1) "LLM-as-analytical-assistant" (Umphrey et al. 2024, above), (2) prompted-LLM-derived text representations for narrative structure (Hatzel & Biemann 2024a), and (3) "concept-specific tracking," where an LLM is used dialogically to determine whether a text relates to a given concept rather than comparing two documents directly (Karjus 2025) — [arXiv:2510.27045](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27045)
- The same survey notes LLMs have also been used to *construct* evaluation/benchmark datasets for intertextuality (generate candidate examples, then have experts verify), rather than to perform detection directly (Fu & Wang 2025; Karjus 2025) — this is a documented instance of LLM-based synthetic data generation adjacent to the "synthetic training data for a smaller retrieval model" role — [arXiv:2510.27045](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27045)
- Membership-inference research establishes that conditioning a target sequence on different prefixes changes token-level log-likelihood in a measurable, exploitable way: ReCaLL (Xie et al., EMNLp 2024, arXiv:2406.15968) shows that prefixing a data point with non-member context induces a larger relative drop in conditional log-likelihood for "member" (memorized/seen) text than for non-member text, and this relative-change signal beats plain perplexity and Min-K%++ by 14.8–15.4% AUC on WikiMIA — this is the closest published analogue to "does conditioning on the candidate source verse reduce per-token surprisal" (the mechanism is the same: compare conditional log-likelihood with vs. without a specific conditioning context), though ReCaLL's target task is training-data membership, not literary borrowing — [ACL Anthology 2024.emnlp-main.493](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.493/) / [arXiv:2406.15968](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15968)
- NLI/entailment approaches for paraphrase and text-similarity detection are a mature model family (DeBERTa-v3-large NLI, multilingual zero-shot NLI models such as BGE-M3-zeroshot based on XLM-RoBERTa) that can be repurposed to score "does target span entail/is entailed by/is paraphrase of source verse" without needing a full generative LLM call — [arxiv.org/html/2212.06933v2 paraphrase review](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.06933v2)

### Inferences
- Given the explicit finding that free-form "LLM outputs candidate pairs" does not scale (Umphrey et al.), the pipeline's existing cheap upstream candidate generator (lemma n-gram, e5 embeddings, dependency-tree overlap) should remain the sole source of candidates; the LLM's role should be constrained to **judging a already-proposed (target-span, source-verse) pair**, not searching cold — this directly matches the project's own architecture already having three signals plus logistic-regression fusion.
- Because no paper reports a validated LLM-as-judge accuracy/agreement number for literary allusion specifically, any deployment must budget for a bespoke small-scale validation against the existing 65-item gold standard (leave-one-out) before trusting LLM judge scores as a fourth feature.
- Conditional-perplexity/surprisal scoring is conceptually transplantable from the MIA literature (ReCaLL-style relative log-likelihood change) into a borrowing-detection score: compute log P(target-span | source-verse-as-context) vs. log P(target-span | neutral/no-context or a random-verse context), and use the relative drop as a length-normalized proxy for "conditioning helps predict this span" — this is a reasonable adaptation to test empirically but is not independently validated for this purpose in the retrieved literature.

### Gaps
- No published study directly measures LLM-as-judge accuracy/agreement specifically for verse-level allusion verification in a modern novel vs. a religious source corpus (Russian or otherwise) — this is a genuine open gap; the closest analogues (biblical Koine Greek, Latin/Greek classical allusion) do not report quantitative judge-vs-expert agreement numbers in the abstracts/summaries retrieved.
- No paper found that runs conditional-perplexity scoring as a *borrowing/paraphrase-detection* signal (as opposed to *training-data membership* detection); the technique's transfer to this new use case is an informed extrapolation, not a confirmed empirical result.

## What does 2023–2026 literature say about LLM-as-judge reliability for literary/intertextuality judgments (agreement, prompt sensitivity, temperature consistency, biases)?

### Takeaway
General-purpose LLM-as-judge reliability research (not literary-specific) consistently finds moderate-to-poor agreement with human experts on subjective/open-ended tasks, strong sensitivity to prompt/label framing (anchoring bias), and non-trivial run-to-run inconsistency even at temperature 0; no literary-allusion-specific or Russian-language-specific LLM-judge reliability study was found, so these general findings must be treated as the best available proxy, with an explicit caveat that literary/intertextual judgment is exactly the "open-ended, low-rubric-anchoring" case where LLM judges are documented to perform worst.
### Cited Findings
- A systematic large-scale evaluation of LLM-as-judge models (arXiv:2606.19544, 2026) finds well-scoped tasks with anchored rubrics reach ~0.89 agreement with human references, while open-ended/subjective tasks drop to ~0.73; raw agreement is highly sensitive to which human reference is used, e.g. a judge reaching 0.83 agreement with one expert dropped to 0.51 agreement with majority-of-experts consensus — [arXiv:2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19544v1)
- The same body of work reports LLM-judge inter-rater agreement (kappa) is more variable and generally lower than human-annotator inter-rater agreement, and cautions that "the contested nature of highly subjective tasks makes it challenging to establish content validity of LLMs as annotators," risking loss of the diversity found in genuine human disagreement — [arXiv:2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19544v1); consistent with "Neither Valid nor Reliable? Investigating the Use of LLMs as Judges" (arXiv:2508.18076)
- Anchoring bias: a 2026 study (arXiv:2608.25869) shows that when an LLM judge is shown prior/reference scores or labels before making its own judgment, this "prior score" anchors the output at shallow transformer layers and resists mitigation; on categorical industry data with human ground truth, anchored metadata blocked 48% of the judge's error corrections and flipped 10.18% of otherwise-correct judgments to the wrong label; neither chain-of-thought prompting nor an explicit "disregard the anchor" warning fully removed the effect — [arXiv:2608.25869](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.25869)
- This anchoring-bias mechanism is directly relevant to the key question about "confirmation/anchoring bias when shown a candidate pair vs. cold search": showing the LLM a proposed (target, source-verse) pair and asking it to confirm/score is architecturally the same "prior label/score shown before judgment" setup implicated in the anchoring-bias literature, implying the design should explicitly test whether presenting the candidate biases the LLM toward confirming it (e.g., compare judge output when the same pair is framed as "confirm this match" vs. "is there any biblical parallel to this passage, and if so which verse" without pre-naming the candidate).
- Temperature/consistency: "The Necessity of Setting Temperature in LLM-as-a-Judge" (arXiv:2603.28304, 2026) finds temperature mainly affects decision variance/reproducibility rather than mean accuracy, and does not change the ranking of verdict candidates but does change the sharpness of the score distribution — [arXiv:2603.28304](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28304v1)
- Multiple studies report LLM judges are not fully deterministic even at temperature 0: variance is large enough in some cases to flip preferred completions between runs; setting temperature to 0 reduced flip-rate by 79% for one model (GPT-4o-mini, 13.3%→2.8%) but only 43% for another (GPT-4.1-mini, to 7.9%); of tested evaluators, Mistral-8x7B was most self-consistent while GPT-4-Turbo showed the largest run-to-run variability (5.7% vote changes); batched inference and MoE routing were identified as sources of nondeterminism even at T=0 — [Same Input, Different Scores](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/401599267_Same_Input_Different_Scores_A_Multi_Model_Study_on_the_Inconsistency_of_LLM_Judge) / general LLM-judge consistency search results
- A related, separately documented bias family is position/order bias: "Judging the Judges: A Systematic Study of Position Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge" (ACL Anthology 2025.ijcnlp-long.18) documents that the order in which candidate items are presented to a judge measurably swings its verdict — relevant if the LLM is ever asked to rank multiple candidate source verses for the same target span rather than score pairs independently — [ACL 2025.ijcnlp-long.18](https://aclanthology.org/2025.ijcnlp-long.18/)

### Inferences
- Because open-ended/subjective judgments show markedly worse LLM-human agreement than rubric-anchored ones, the practical implementation implication is to force the LLM into a narrow, rubric-anchored scoring task (e.g., a fixed 0–3 ordinal scale with explicit written criteria per level: "0 = no relation, 1 = shared theme/vocabulary only, 2 = probable paraphrase/echo, 3 = clear quotation/close paraphrase") rather than an open "is this an allusion, yes/no" or free-text judgment — this follows directly from the rubric-vs-open-ended agreement gap reported above.
- Given documented anchoring effects, a defensible evaluation design contrasts two prompting modes: (a) "verification" mode — show the LLM the (target span, source verse) pair and ask it to score confidence that the source is being echoed; (b) "blind/cold" mode — show only the target span plus a small shortlist of plausible verses (or ask semantic-search-style within a limited context window) without indicating which one the upstream pipeline flagged, and check whether scores/rankings diverge between modes as a bias diagnostic.
- Given nondeterminism even at T=0, any LLM score used as a pipeline feature should be run multiple times (e.g., 3–5 samples) and averaged/majority-voted, and the implementation should log the exact prompt, model quantization, and Ollama/llama.cpp version, since batching and backend nondeterminism can shift results between runs even with fixed decoding parameters.

### Gaps
- No literary-allusion-specific or Russian-language-specific LLM-judge reliability paper was found (searches for "LLM-as-judge literary humanities agreement," "LLM-as-judge Russian" type queries returned only general-domain and non-Russian-specific results); this is an open gap the engineer should treat as a warning rather than fill with an untested assumption.
- No paper was found reporting inter-run consistency specifically for a 7B, Ollama-served, open-weight model (Qwen2.5-7B or similar) doing pairwise text-similarity/allusion judgments; findings above are from GPT-4-class and mixed proprietary/open evaluator studies, so numeric flip-rates should not be assumed to transfer directly to Qwen2.5-7B at this scale.

## Does conditional-perplexity/surprisal outperform embedding cosine similarity for paraphrastic reuse, and what is the compute feasibility at 7B/12GB VRAM for tens of thousands of pairs?

### Takeaway
There is no published head-to-head comparison of conditional-perplexity/surprisal scoring against embedding cosine similarity specifically for paraphrastic textual-reuse/allusion detection; the supporting evidence is indirect, drawn from the membership-inference literature (where conditional log-likelihood signals reliably beat raw perplexity for detecting "this text influenced the model's/context's prediction") and from generic local-LLM throughput benchmarks, which suggest the compute is feasible for hundreds-to-low-thousands of candidate pairs per run on a single RTX 3060 12GB but not for evaluating the full 15,000–750,000-pair candidate space without an aggressive upstream filter.
### Cited Findings
- No direct study comparing conditional perplexity/surprisal vs. embedding cosine similarity for paraphrase/text-reuse detection was found in the search results retrieved; the closest indirectly relevant technique, ReCaLL, uses the *relative change* in conditional log-likelihood (not raw perplexity) as its discriminative signal and shows this outperforms raw perplexity-based baselines by a wide margin (14.8–15.4% AUC over Min-K%++) for the related but distinct task of pretraining-data membership inference — [arXiv:2406.15968](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15968)
- On compute feasibility: at Q4_K_M quantization, a 7B model occupies roughly 4–5 GB of VRAM, leaving headroom on a 12 GB card for KV cache and context; reported single-batch throughput for 7B-8B Q4 models on an RTX 3060 12GB is in the range of ~30–65 tokens/sec depending on source and configuration (one benchmark cites ~52 tok/s, another ~45 tok/s, a third a 30–50 tok/s range) — [llmrun.dev RTX 3060](https://llmrun.dev/gpu/rtx-3060-12gb), [modelfit.io RTX 3060](https://modelfit.io/gpu/rtx-3060/), [singhajit.com benchmark](https://singhajit.com/llm-inference-speed-comparison/)
- llama.cpp's server (the engine underlying Ollama) supports continuous/dynamic batching by default, and Ollama has added `logprob_tokens` support to report log-probabilities for caller-named tokens on the llama.cpp backend, computing a full-vocab log-softmax before top-K truncation for the native runner — this is the API surface needed to extract per-token surprisal/conditional log-likelihood efficiently without a full external re-implementation — [GitHub PR ollama/ollama#18580](https://github.com/ollama/ollama/pull/18580), [llama.cpp server README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/server/README.md)

### Inferences
- Assuming ~40–50 tok/s single-stream throughput and that a conditional-perplexity check requires only a forward pass over the target span (tens of tokens) conditioned on a short verse context (also tens of tokens) — i.e., no autoregressive generation, just one forward pass per pair — the per-pair cost is dominated by prompt processing (prefill), not decode; prefill on 7B/Q4 llama.cpp backends is typically markedly faster than decode token-by-token (often several hundred tok/s for prefill on consumer GPUs, though no source specifically measured for RTX 3060 was retrieved), suggesting that scoring low thousands of candidate pairs (e.g., a shortlisted top-20-100 per target passage across the corpus) is plausible within a batch job of minutes to a few hours on this hardware, but scoring the full 15,000–750,000-pair space is very unlikely to be practical without either much heavier batching/parallelism engineering than a naive loop, or restricting the LLM stage to a small shortlist per passage (which is exactly the funnel architecture the project already uses upstream).
- Because Ollama's OpenAI-compatible endpoint is designed for one-request-at-a-time-style chat completion rather than large-batch scoring, an engineer wanting real throughput for logprob-based scoring over thousands of pairs should consider calling llama.cpp's server directly (or a batching-aware server like vLLM, which the search results mention as an alternative local-serving option) rather than routing everything through Ollama's default single-turn chat API, since continuous batching in llama.cpp's server is a documented default-on feature that Ollama's simpler API surface may not fully expose to naive client code.

### Gaps
- No source quantified prefill vs. decode throughput specifically for an RTX 3060 12GB, so the "minutes to hours" estimate above is an inference from general architecture knowledge (prefill is parallel over the prompt, decode is sequential) rather than a directly cited benchmark; the engineer should benchmark this directly for their exact Ollama/Qwen2.5-7B setup before committing to a batch-size plan.
- No study was found establishing conditional-perplexity as either superior or inferior to embedding cosine similarity for detecting paraphrastic (not verbatim) textual reuse in any language; this is a genuinely open empirical question that the project would need to test itself against its own 65-item gold standard.

## How do practitioners architect a cheap candidate generator feeding a smaller LLM re-ranker/verifier (avoiding full candidate-space LLM scoring)?

### Takeaway
The standard, well-documented architecture is a two-(or three-)stage retrieve-then-rerank pipeline — cheap bi-encoder/lexical retrieval producing a top-K shortlist (commonly K=20–100), followed by a more expensive cross-encoder and/or LLM-based reranker applied only to that shortlist — and published tuning work finds ranking-quality gains plateau at surprisingly shallow K, meaning a small shortlist per query captures most of the achievable quality at a fraction of the cost of scoring everything.
### Cited Findings
- The canonical two-stage pattern: a first-stage retriever (hybrid or dense) retrieves top-K candidates (K≈20 commonly cited), then a cross-encoder (e.g., BAAI/bge-reranker-base, cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2) scores the query jointly with each candidate document, which is described as "ideal for re-ranking a small candidate set (typically k = 20-100)" due to cross-encoders' higher per-pair cost — [Medium: Reranking using Cross Encoder](https://medium.com/@aishikbhattacharjee98/reranking-using-cross-encoder-boost-your-rag-pipeline-accuracy-d2da22006dad), [CustomGPT RAG reranking techniques](https://customgpt.ai/rag-reranking-techniques/)
- One retrieved source reports a tuning sweep in which "the robust score peaks at a remarkably shallow depth of K=50," allowing a production pipeline to rerank only the top 50 candidates while preserving most of the achievable ranking quality — directly supporting keeping the LLM-facing shortlist small (tens, not thousands, of items per target passage) — [RAG reranking techniques discussion, CustomGPT.ai source cluster]
- A documented "hybrid reranker" pattern cascades a cross-encoder stage (cheap, narrows a larger candidate set) followed by an LLM reranking stage (more expensive, reasons over the already-narrowed set), explicitly framed as a way to "achieve higher overall accuracy without incurring the full computational cost of applying LLM reasoning to large candidate sets" — this maps directly onto layering an LLM verifier on top of the project's existing three-signal (lexical/embedding/dependency-graph) candidate generator plus logistic-regression fusion, rather than replacing it — [search synthesis, cross-encoder/LLM reranking sources above]
- The PAN 2025 generative plagiarism detection task (Greiner-Petter, Fröbe, Wahle, Ruas, Gipp, Aizawa, Potthast; PAN@CLEF 2025, arXiv:2510.06805) is architecturally a source-retrieval-plus-text-alignment task at corpus scale (dataset of 78,038 document pairs generated by Llama, DeepSeek-R1, and Mistral), and reports that competitive submissions using basic semantic-similarity/embedding approaches reached up to 0.8 recall and 0.5 precision on the new dataset, but that most of these approaches "underperform significantly" when evaluated on the older PAN 2015 dataset, indicating a generalizability gap between embedding-based similarity and true robustness to varied obfuscation/paraphrase styles — this is a directly relevant shared-task precedent for the project's own retrieve-then-verify architecture and its stated goal of catching paraphrastic, not just verbatim, reuse — [arXiv:2510.06805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06805)

### Inferences
- Given the project's existing candidate counts (15,000–750,000 pairs depending on layer) and the shallow-K plateau finding (K≈50 capturing most quality), a defensible design is: keep all three existing cheap signals (lemma n-gram, e5 embedding, dependency-graph overlap) plus the logistic-regression fusion score exactly as-is to produce a per-target-passage ranked list, then send only the top-20 to top-50 ranked source verses per target passage to the LLM verifier — this keeps total LLM calls in the low tens-of-thousands range even across the full novel, which is compatible with the throughput estimates above.
- The PAN 2025 finding that embedding-only approaches generalize poorly across obfuscation styles is a direct piece of supporting evidence for adding a genuinely different signal (LLM judgment, conditional perplexity) rather than relying solely on refining the existing embedding-based method, since the shared-task literature shows embedding similarity alone has a documented ceiling on paraphrase-style reuse.

### Gaps
- No source specifically addresses optimal shortlist size (K) for a *literary allusion* task (as opposed to generic RAG or scientific-plagiarism retrieval), so the K≈20–50 guidance is transferred from adjacent domains, not validated for this corpus's specific length/pair-count profile.

## What are documented failure modes and contamination risks specific to this task, and how are they tested/mitigated?

### Takeaway
The dominant, well-documented risk category is data/benchmark contamination — the LLM may have memorized scholarly commentary explicitly linking this specific novel to specific Bible verses — and the standard mitigation toolkit (membership-inference-style perplexity/log-likelihood tests, canary/perturbation tests, held-out or paraphrased-probe evaluation) is mature in the general contamination-detection literature, though not validated specifically for literary-allusion attribution; the intertextuality-specific literature separately documents LLM hallucination of false/spurious intertextual dependencies as an independent, non-contamination-related failure mode requiring expert review.
### Cited Findings
- Umphrey, Roberts & Roberts (arXiv:2409.01882) explicitly report that their LLM pipeline "introduces false textual dependencies" (i.e., hallucinated intertextual links) in addition to struggling with long input passages, and state that expert evaluation is necessary specifically because of this — an independently documented failure mode distinct from contamination — [arXiv:2409.01882](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.01882)
- General contamination-detection literature frames the problem as an instance of membership inference: "detecting data contamination can be regarded as a specific instance of membership inference attacks," and the intuition underlying most detection methods is that lower perplexity/higher log-likelihood on a passage suggests the model saw that passage (or closely related text) during training — [survey references retrieved: arXiv:2510.09259, dl.acm.org/10.1145/3779430]
- Detection methodology in this space splits into two families: memorization-extraction methods (checking for verbatim or near-verbatim reproduction of specific text, e.g., prompting the model with a fragment and checking whether it completes it correctly) and statistical membership-inference methods (using perplexity/confidence-score anomalies, e.g., Min-K%++, ReCaLL) — both families are directly adaptable as a contamination *test* for this project: (a) prompt Qwen2.5-7B with the opening of the specific gold-standard verse-citation pairs and see if it can complete/name the source without being told, and (b) compare its log-likelihood on the exact known scholarly-annotated pairs vs. score on deliberately fabricated/plausible-but-false (novel-span, verse) pairs, looking for suspiciously confident or "too clean" scores on the true positives — [arXiv:2510.09259](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.09259), [ACM Computing Surveys 10.1145/3779430](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3779430)
- ReCaLL's finding that member (seen) text shows a distinctively different response to added context/prefix compared to non-member text (arXiv:2406.15968) suggests a concrete contamination probe for this project: run the LLM-verifier scoring procedure not just on the 65 known gold-standard pairs but on a matched set of synthetic/held-out pairs the annotators did not select (same novel, plausible-but-unattested candidate verses) and check whether the LLM's scores on the *known, published-in-scholarship* pairs are anomalously higher/more confident than its scores on hardness-matched negatives — a gap between these would be evidence the LLM is pattern-matching on memorized scholarly association rather than reasoning about actual textual similarity — [arXiv:2406.15968](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15968)

### Inferences
- Because the gold standard is only 65 items and was very likely drawn from, or overlaps with, published scholarly commentary on this specific 19th-century Russian novel (a canonical, well-studied text), the contamination risk here is unusually high relative to a generic NLP benchmark — the novel-and-verse pairing is exactly the kind of citable, discussable literary fact that would appear in Russian-language secondary literature (essays, annotated editions, commentaries) plausibly included in Qwen2.5's training data. This raises the practical recommendation to treat any LLM-judge accuracy number on the existing 65-item gold standard with real suspicion, and to construct a second, harder evaluation set of the same character but drawn from either (a) less-studied/obscure passages of the same novel not covered in standard commentary, or (b) genuinely fabricated/scrambled (target-span, verse) pairs that are textually similar but not the scholarly-attested match, to isolate genuine text-comparison ability from memorized association.
- The "false intertextual dependency" hallucination risk (Umphrey et al.) is a separate concern from contamination and argues for keeping the LLM in a narrow verification role (score a given pair) rather than an open generative role (find/propose links), since open generation is precisely where hallucinated links were observed.

### Gaps
- No published study was found that specifically tests or quantifies contamination for the pairing of a *specific well-known 19th-century Russian novel* with the *Synodal Bible*; the contamination-test methodology above is adapted from general LLM contamination-detection literature and would need to be run empirically by the project team, not merely cited.

## Are there published applications of LLMs to Church Slavonic / Old Russian / archaic-register normalization as pipeline preprocessing?

### Takeaway
There is an active but small and very recent (2025–2026) research community applying both traditional NLP tools and LLMs to Old Church Slavonic and pre-modern Slavic texts, but published results show these models perform poorly out-of-the-box on this register, and no source found describes using such normalization as a preprocessing step specifically feeding into a *modern-language* borrowing/allusion pipeline like this project's (Synodal Bible, which is already in modern Russian rather than Church Slavonic, somewhat reduces the direct relevance of this concern for the current corpus).
### Cited Findings
- "Towards Benchmarking Old Church Slavonic Lemmatization" (ACL Anthology 2026.brigap-1.9) evaluates lemmatization tools including Stanza, UDPipe-2, and dictionary-based approaches on Old Church Slavonic, and reports pretrained models perform poorly (≈15–16% accuracy), below even a simple dictionary baseline (≈38%), while retraining on new annotated data raises performance up to ≈51% — indicating off-the-shelf modern NLP/LLM tooling transfers very poorly to this register without dedicated retraining — [ACL Anthology 2026.brigap-1.9](https://aclanthology.org/2026.brigap-1.9/)
- "Messy data, low-resource languages, and LLMs: Narrative analysis of pre-modern Slavic Lives of Saints" (Computational Humanities Research journal, Cambridge Core) explicitly frames the challenge: "pre-modern Slavic texts pose particular difficulties due to rich morphology, orthographic variation, and limited standardization, which complicate the application of both traditional NLP tools and off-the-shelf LLMs" — a direct, citable statement that generic LLMs (presumably including 7B-class open models) are not well-suited to this register without adaptation — [Cambridge Core CHR article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/computational-humanities-research/article/messy-data-lowresource-languages-and-llms-narrative-analysis-of-premodern-slavic-lives-of-saints/5F4C1C3F035B94DF5491DB38A53FC86C)
- A 2025 approach explores normalizing/harmonizing modern Slavic language data's orthography and morphology to imitate Early Slavic forms, leveraging resources such as the Tromsø Old Church Slavonic and Old Russian Treebank (TOROT) combined with modern-language data — this is a normalization-as-preprocessing strategy, but described as a data-augmentation/training strategy for building better OCS-specific tools, not as an LLM-based preprocessing stage feeding a downstream modern-language borrowing-detection pipeline — [ACL Anthology bsnlp 2025.bsnlp-1.12](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/pdf/bsnlp/2025.bsnlp-1.12.pdf)
- Older hybrid approaches (rule-based + dictionary + seq2seq neural network) exist specifically for Old Church Slavonic lemmatization (Afanasev, HSE working paper) predating the LLM era, establishing that archaic-register normalization has historically been treated as a distinct NLP subtask requiring specialized (non-LLM) tooling — [HSE working paper, Afanasev](https://wp.hse.ru/data/2021/02/18/1393879077/106LNG2021.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because the project's reference corpus is the Synodal Bible — a 19th-century *modern literary Russian* translation, not Church Slavonic — the archaic-register-normalization problem documented in these papers is likely a secondary concern for this specific pipeline rather than a blocking one; it would become directly relevant only if the project later extended to liturgical/Church-Slavonic source texts, or if the 19th-century novel itself contains Church-Slavonicisms/archaisms in quoted or allusive passages that a modern-register-tuned e5 embedding model or Qwen2.5 tokenizer might handle poorly. Given the poor reported performance of both traditional NLP tools and off-the-shelf LLMs on true Church Slavonic (≈15–16% lemmatization accuracy without retraining), any future extension toward genuinely Church-Slavonic source material should not assume Qwen2.5-7B handles it well without dedicated evaluation.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that directly integrates LLM-based archaic-register normalization as an upstream preprocessing step feeding into a modern-language allusion/text-reuse detection pipeline of the kind this project runs; this exact combination appears to be an unaddressed gap in the current literature, so the project could not lean on precedent here and would need to prototype and validate this step itself if it becomes necessary.
