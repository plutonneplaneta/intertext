# Statistical & Evaluation-Methodology Best Practices for Small-Gold-Standard / Large-Unverified-Negative-Space Detection Systems

Scope note: this covers confound removal beyond quantile z-scoring, FDR/precision estimation under unverified negatives, small-N cross-validation for an ensemble score, sensitivity analyses for an incomplete gold standard, and multiple-comparisons correction under correlated tests — for the biblical-citation-detection pilot (31,102-verse Synodal Bible corpus, 65-row/46-unique-passage gold standard from one commentary, 3 signals with 15k–750k candidate pairs each).

## Key Question 1: Beyond quantile-binned z-scoring, what established techniques exist for removing a continuous confound (length) from a similarity/overlap score, especially under heavy-tailed backgrounds?

### Takeaway
Binning is a special (crude) case of a more general family of confound-removal techniques; the literature offers at least four better-grounded alternatives — regression-based residualization (ideally quantile regression, not OLS, since backgrounds are heavy-tailed), continuous/smooth norming (quantile regression or GAMLSS-style models used in psychometrics as the direct replacement for age-binned norms), permutation/shuffle-based empirical null distributions, and explicit "number of trials" null models from sequence-alignment statistics (Karlin-Altschul/BLAST theory) that model the expected maximum score as a function of the number of independent comparisons (n-grams or edges tested) rather than as an empirical bin mean/SD.

### Cited Findings
- Length is explicitly recognized in the similarity/IR literature as a confound on vector-based similarity scores; there is even a patented "system and method for handling the confounding effect of document length on vector-based similarity scores" — confirming this is a known, named problem, not specific to this pipeline — [USPTO patent](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9311390).
- Standard residualization approach: regress the score (or reward) on the confound (e.g., log-length) and subtract the fitted component to get a residual that is (ideally) confound-free; a documented failure mode is that this "scalar" residualization is often insufficient alone, and per-dimension or partial-correlation approaches (regressing out the confound from both target and prediction, not just one side) explain more of the confound's variance than one-sided residualization — [When Residualization Helps an Audit (arXiv 2609.24194)](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24194); [bioRxiv confound-control ML paper](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.08.17.255034v1.full).
- "Continuous norming" via **quantile regression** is an established alternative to binning a continuous covariate (age, in the psychometric literature) into discrete groups before computing norms/percentiles. It models the conditional τ-th percentile directly as τ_q(x) = β₀ + β·x (or a nonlinear/spline function of x) rather than approximating group means/SDs; it is fit by minimizing weighted absolute residuals (not squared error), which is inherently more robust to non-normal, heavy-tailed, skewed distributions than a per-bin mean/SD z-score. It also removes the "edge of cohort" artifact where two paragraphs of nearly identical length land in different bins and get different normalized scores, and it is more sample-efficient than binning (traditional per-bin norming needs roughly 300+ observations per bin for stable estimates) — [Bias and Precision of Continuous Norms Obtained Using Quantile Regression, PMC8392776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8392776/).
- Caveat on quantile regression for norming: it can introduce systematic bias when the underlying score distribution has ceiling/floor effects or strong skew, and the paper's own finding was that bias "depended on the subgroup and percentile combination" when linearity assumptions were violated — recommending it works best when distributions are approximately symmetric (or transformed to be so, e.g. via log) — [same source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8392776/).
- Sequence-alignment statistics (Karlin-Altschul theory underlying BLAST) provide a rigorous "number of trials" null model directly analogous to n-gram/edge-overlap counting against a large corpus: the maximal local-alignment (or in this case, overlap) score between random sequences follows an extreme-value (Gumbel) distribution, not a Gaussian, and the number of high-scoring regions above a threshold is modeled as Poisson. The E-value formula E = K·m·n·exp(-λ·S) explicitly incorporates the "opportunity" size (m = query/passage length, n = database size) so that longer queries or larger databases are corrected for exactly because they mechanically produce more chances for a high score by chance — this is a closer match to the paper's own diagnosis ("longer passages mechanically accumulate higher scores... independent of genuine borrowing") than any binned z-score — [SequenceServer explainer](https://sequenceserver.com/blog/blast-e-value-meaning/); [Karlin-Altschul statistics, CS182 Brown notes](https://cs.brown.edu/courses/csci1820/spring-2022/notes/CH1_Notes.pdf); [drive5.com summary](https://www.drive5.com/usearch/manual/karlin_altschul.html).
- BLAST additionally corrects for length using an "effective length" adjustment, Length_effective = max(Length_real − λ·S/H, 1), where H is the relative entropy of the scoring scheme — a length correction computed from first principles rather than empirically binned — [same sources].
- General extreme-value-theory (EVT) framing for retrieval: relevance of top-ranked results in a similarity-search system can be predicted from the score alone using EVT, treating the search-space size explicitly; under sub-Gaussian tail assumptions the expected maximum of many draws is bounded by the baseline mean plus a term proportional to √(log(number of comparisons)) — directly generalizable to "how high should the top score be, by chance, for a passage that gets N n-gram/edge tests against a 31,102-verse corpus?" — [Relevance prediction in similarity-search systems using extreme value theory, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1047320319300720).
- Permutation/shuffle-based empirical null models are an established technique in intertextuality and text-reuse research specifically: null models built by shuffling word/n-gram order (preserving unigram frequencies but destroying local structure) are used to establish what the base statistical properties of n-gram overlap/matching look like in the absence of genuine structure — [arXiv 2509.06637, Modelling Intertextuality with N-gram Embeddings](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.06637); survey confirming null-hypothesis-testing approaches used generally in reuse/copy-detection research to "refute claims of copy by chance" — [Estimating a Null Model of Scientific Image Reuse, arXiv 2003.00878](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.00878); general survey — [Quantitative Intertextuality from the Digital Humanities Perspective: A Survey, arXiv 2510.27045](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.27045).

### Inferences
- The pipeline's quantile-binned z-scoring is a coarse, discretized instance of "continuous norming" from psychometrics; replacing the 10 discrete length-quantile bins with a quantile regression (or GAM/spline) of score-percentile on log-length, fit on the presumed-negative background, would remove the discretization/edge-of-bin artifact and should further reduce (not necessarily eliminate) the residual r≈0.07–0.12 correlation, because it uses a smooth, robust-to-outliers fit rather than per-bin sample mean/SD (which is exactly what struggles with heavy tails/small per-bin samples).
- Given the pipeline already has, for at least the n-gram and dependency-edge signals, a natural count of "opportunities tested" (number of n-grams in a paragraph × number of n-grams in the 31,102-verse corpus, or number of dependency edges compared), an EVT/Poisson-style null model (Karlin-Altschul-style) is conceptually the most principled option because it is derived from the actual generative mechanism of the score (a max/top-order-statistic over many weak comparisons) rather than being an empirical curve fit. This would let significance be expressed as an E-value/p-value under an explicit combinatorial null, which also directly supports Key Question 5 (multiple-comparisons correction) since E-values are already "expected number of comparably-scoring hits under the null," i.e., pre-corrected for multiplicity in a sense analogous to Bonferroni/FDR.
- For the embedding-cosine-similarity signal (which is not a simple count-based overlap), residualization (regress cosine score on log-length, or on the count of tokens/sentences embedded) or continuous-norming quantile regression is more directly applicable than an EVT/Poisson model, since cosine similarity is not literally a "max over discrete trials" — the three signals likely need different confound-removal techniques matched to their generative structure, not one uniform method.

### Gaps
- No source directly addresses residualizing/normalizing *sentence-embedding cosine similarity* against passage length specifically (as opposed to alignment or n-gram scores); this seems to be a genuine gap in the literature the researchers found — general confound-removal papers exist but none specific to embedding similarity vs. text length was located in this search pass.
- Did not find a source directly combining EVT/Poisson null modeling *and* dependency-edge overlap (a graph/parse-based similarity), only general EVT-for-retrieval and Karlin-Altschul (built for sequence alignment) — applying the Karlin-Altschul machinery to a dependency-edge-overlap score would require a novel derivation of λ and K for that scoring scheme, which is a research task in itself, not an out-of-the-box tool.

## Key Question 2: How should FDR/precision/confidence be estimated and reported when negatives are presumed, not verified? PU-learning and PAN guidance.

### Takeaway
This is precisely the setting formalized by positive-unlabeled (PU) learning, which provides principled estimators (Elkan & Noto 2008, and successors) for recovering true precision/FDR from PU-observed counts under an explicit assumption (SCAR — "selected completely at random" — meaning the labeled positives are a random sample of all true positives) that should be stated and defended (or its violation flagged) in any report; PAN's plagiarism-detection protocol and TREC's bpref/incomplete-qrels literature offer a complementary, assumption-light approach: report metrics that are robust to unjudged/unlabeled items rather than trying to correct point estimates.

### Cited Findings
- The foundational PU-learning result (Elkan & Noto, 2008): under the SCAR assumption (positively-labeled examples are drawn independently of their features, at random from the true positive population, i.e., no reason a genuine allusion would be *more* likely to have been caught by the 19th-century commentary than another), a classifier trained to distinguish "labeled" vs. "unlabeled" approximates the true positive posterior up to a constant equal to the label frequency c = P(labeled | positive); c can be estimated from the average predicted "labeled" probability on a held-out set of known positives, and then used to rescale the non-traditional classifier into a true positive/negative posterior — [Recovering True Classifier Performance in Positive-Unlabeled Learning, arXiv 1702.00518](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.00518); [Class-prior estimation for learning from positive and unlabeled data, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-016-5604-6); survey — [Learning from positive and unlabeled data: a survey, arXiv 1811.04820 / Machine Learning journal](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-020-05877-5).
- SCAR is explicitly flagged in the literature as often unrealistic and has known extensions for when it fails ("Selected At Random" but not "completely" at random, i.e., propensity of being labeled can depend on features) — relevant here because a scholar's commentary is *more* likely to have flagged long, well-known, or thematically prominent passages, which is a plausible violation of SCAR (labeling propensity may correlate with exactly the length confound under study) — [Learning from Positive and Unlabeled Data under the Selected At Random Assumption, arXiv 1808.08755](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.08755); [Positive Unlabeled Learning Selected Not At Random (PULSNAR), PMC11622864](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11622864/).
- The false-positive rate under PU learning cannot be computed directly (no verified-negative sample exists), but it can be re-expressed in terms of the "PU false positive rate" (rate of unlabeled items scored positive) combined with the estimated class prior/label frequency — giving a formula-based route to an *estimated* FDR rather than a naive one that treats all non-gold items as confirmed negative — [False positive rate control for positive unlabeled learning, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231219311051); [Estimating classification accuracy in positive-unlabeled learning: characterization and correction strategies, PMC6417800](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6417800/).
- PAN (the standard shared-task venue for plagiarism/text-reuse detection) evaluates with macro-averaged precision, recall, granularity, and a combined "plagdet" score computed from exact character-span overlaps between system output and a constructed truth set; macro-averaging (length-independent) is preferred over micro-averaging (length-weighted) specifically because micro-averaged variants are considered "less indicative" once the reference standard is not exhaustively fine-grained — a directly transferable idea: report macro-averaged (per-target-passage) precision/recall rather than micro-averaged (per-candidate-pair) precision/recall, so that a handful of paragraph pairs with huge candidate counts don't dominate the metric — [Overview of the Plagiarism Detection Task at PAN 2025, arXiv 2510.06805 / CEUR-WS](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.06805v1).
- In classic IR, pooling-based evaluation with incomplete relevance judgments is a decades-old, precisely analogous problem (only a "pool" of documents get manually judged; everything else is presumed non-relevant, exactly like this pipeline's presumed-negative paragraphs), and pooling bias is well documented: it disadvantages/underestimates the true performance of systems whose top hits were not part of what got manually judged — [Reliable Information Retrieval Evaluation with Incomplete and Biased Judgements, Büttcher & Clarke, SIGIR 2007 (dl.acm.org)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1277741.1277755).
- **bpref** (Buckley & Voorhees, TREC 2005 terabyte track) is the standard IR metric designed exactly for this situation: instead of treating unjudged documents as irrelevant, it computes a metric using *only* the judged (labeled) documents, comparing relative ranks of judged-relevant vs. judged-irrelevant items, and is explicitly "independent of how the unjudged documents are retrieved" — i.e., it refuses to penalize or reward a system based on presumed-negative items at all, sidestepping the false-negative-labeling problem entirely rather than trying to correct for it. bpref correlates very highly with mean average precision (MAP) under complete judgments but diverges as judgment completeness drops, especially at very sparse judgment levels — [Reliable IR Evaluation paper, dl.acm.org](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/1277741.1277755); corroborating summary via Semantic Scholar and additional discussion of pooling/bpref history — [Pooling-based continuous evaluation of IR systems, Discover Computing/Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10791-015-9266-y).

### Inferences
- Given the domain expert (commentary author) plausibly had a labeling propensity correlated with passage salience/length — which is the same confound already identified as biasing the raw scores — the SCAR assumption underlying the simplest PU correction (Elkan-Noto) is questionable here and should be explicitly flagged as a limitation if that correction is used; a PULSNAR/SAR-aware variant, or simply not attempting a point-estimate FDR correction and instead reporting a bpref-style rank-based metric plus a qualitative sensitivity bound (see Key Question 4), is the more honest choice for a report given the very small (46-passage) label set, which is too small to reliably estimate a class-prior/propensity model on its own.
- The most actionable, low-risk-of-overclaiming combination for this pipeline's report is: (a) report bpref-style metrics (or an analogous "ignore unjudged, compare only judged pairs" ranking metric) as the primary honest number, since it needs no unverifiable assumption about the negative pool; (b) additionally report a PU-style *bounded estimate* of precision/FDR under an explicit, stated SCAR-type assumption, framed as a sensitivity/what-if number rather than a point estimate; (c) never report "precision" or "FDR" computed by treating 100% of non-gold candidates as confirmed-negative without this caveat, since that is the one practice flagged as *not* defensible as-is.

### Gaps
- No PAN paper specifically addresses the case of an intentionally non-exhaustive/small gold standard (PAN's plagiarism corpora are synthetically constructed to be exhaustive by design, unlike a scholar's printed commentary) — so PAN's protocol is a useful metric-design analogy but not a direct precedent for "gold standard built from one non-exhaustive human source." This gap should be reported as such rather than implying PAN validates this exact scenario.
- Did not find a paper applying PU learning specifically to citation/allusion detection in literary corpora (as opposed to generic PU-learning methodology or generic plagiarism detection); the recommendation to use PU-learning framing here is an extrapolation from the general methodology literature, not a domain-specific precedent.

## Key Question 3: Best practices for leave-one-out/small-N cross-validation of a combined/ensemble score (here: ~16–25 evaluable groups)

### Takeaway
With on the order of 15–25 evaluable groups, leave-one-group-out CV is an appropriate and standard choice (better than a held-out split, which would waste already-scarce labels), but the single point estimate it produces is known to be unstable and should always be accompanied by a resampling-based (bootstrap or permutation) uncertainty interval; plain LOOCV is also known to have specific statistical pathologies (bias in certain metrics, non-smoothness) that make an accompanying bootstrap especially important rather than optional.

### Cited Findings
- LOOCV is specifically recommended as usable "even with small samples where other techniques such as tenfold or fivefold CV might run into problems," and it uses nearly all data to fit each model, making it less likely to overestimate test error compared to a single held-out validation split — [Medium/CodeX summary of resampling methods](https://medium.com/codex/two-resampling-approaches-to-assess-a-model-cross-validation-and-bootstrap-794916d37212); general small-sample CV guidance — [s41512-023-00146-0, Diagnostic and Prognostic Research](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s41512-023-00146-0).
- However, cross-validation and bootstrapping are both flagged in the literature as "unreliable in small sample classification" as point estimates on their own — the estimate itself carries high variance and can look artifactually good or bad depending on which single case is held out — [Cross-validation and bootstrapping are unreliable in small sample classification (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222650078_Cross-validation_and_bootstrapping_are_unreliable_in_small_sample_classification).
- LOOCV has documented specific statistical pathologies beyond generic small-N noise: it has been shown to induce **negative bias into the c-statistic** (a ranking/discrimination metric closely related to AUC, relevant to a ranking-style evaluation of this pipeline's combined score), and a 2024 paper demonstrates "distributional bias" that compromises LOOCV more generally — meaning a single LOOCV point estimate for a ranking-style metric should not be taken as unbiased without correction — [Distributional bias compromises leave-one-out cross-validation, arXiv 2406.01652](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.01652).
- The standard remedy is to wrap the resampling procedure in an outer bootstrap: bootstrapping the out-of-sample (or leave-one-out) predictions themselves, rather than relying on the single realized CV point estimate, yields confidence intervals for the performance metric; the literature suggests on the order of 50–100 bootstrap resamples is enough for a leave-one-out-bootstrap (LOOB) estimator to give reliable interval estimates — [Bootstrapping the out-of-sample predictions for efficient and accurate cross-validation, PMC6191021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6191021/).
- Permutation tests are specifically recommended over asymptotic/parametric tests exactly in the small-sample regime, because "theoretical distribution functions fail to provide satisfactory fits" and permutation-based solutions "maintain nominal test size due to their intrinsic exactness even in cases of small sample sizes... not sufficient for asymptotic approximations" — directly applicable to testing whether the ensemble's observed rank improvement over any single signal could arise by chance with only ~16–25 groups — [Permutation tests are robust and powerful at 0.5% and 5% significance levels, Behavior Research Methods](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-021-01595-5); general review — [A review of multivariate permutation tests, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047259X25000168).

### Inferences
- For this pipeline, the recommended combination is: (1) keep leave-one-target-out CV as the point-estimate procedure (already correctly chosen given the label scarcity); (2) wrap it in a bootstrap over the *set of evaluable target passages* (resample the ~16 target groups with replacement, rerun LOO-CV-based scoring on each resample) to get a percentile or BCa confidence interval on whatever headline ranking metric is reported (e.g., mean reciprocal rank, top-k accuracy) — this directly answers "could the point estimate be an artifact of the specific small sample?"; (3) run a label-permutation test (shuffle which paragraphs are "gold" among the pool of plausible candidates, or permute the target-passage labels across the negative pool) to get a null distribution for the observed improvement of the combined/logistic-regression score over the best single signal, and report a permutation p-value alongside the point estimate, rather than presenting the LOO point estimate alone as if it were a reliable, low-variance number.
- Given the documented specific bias of LOOCV on discrimination/ranking metrics (c-statistic-like), the report should explicitly flag that the leave-one-target-out point estimate could carry a systematic (not just high-variance) bias, and should not be presented as a clean unbiased number even after bootstrapping the interval — bootstrapping fixes the "how much could this vary" question but not necessarily the "is this centered correctly" question.

### Gaps
- Did not find a study specifically quantifying LOOCV bias for *ranking* metrics like mean reciprocal rank or top-k accuracy (as opposed to the c-statistic/AUC-like metrics that were directly documented) — this is a related but not identical situation, and applying the c-statistic-bias finding to a rank-based metric is an extrapolation the report should flag as such.
- No Bayesian small-sample estimation source was retrieved with sufficient specificity in this search pass (e.g., a Bayesian hierarchical model treating each of the ~16 target passages as a group with partial pooling) — this is a plausible and often-recommended alternative to bootstrap/permutation for small-N settings (partial pooling reduces the variance problem by borrowing strength across groups) but was not directly corroborated with a citation in this research pass and should be flagged as a recommendation made on general Bayesian-statistics principles rather than a specific retrieved source.

## Key Question 4: Recommended sensitivity analyses / robustness checks for an incomplete gold standard

### Takeaway
The core, directly transferable technique from IR evaluation is to explicitly quantify how much the reported metrics would change if some presumed-negatives were reclassified as unlabeled/unknown rather than confirmed-negative (this is the "bpref" philosophy generalized into a sensitivity sweep) — i.e., re-run the evaluation multiple times while treating the top-N highest-scoring "false positives" as excluded/unknown rather than confirmed-negative, and report the resulting range of the metric as an honesty-preserving bound rather than a single number.

### Cited Findings
- The IR literature's core finding motivating bpref and related work is that standard precision/recall-style measures are demonstrably *not robust* to incomplete judgments — performance estimates measurably shift as the judgment set becomes less complete, and this shift has been directly quantified in TREC-style studies (Buckley & Voorhees; and subsequent comparative studies of multiple incomplete-judgment-robust measures) — [Retrieval evaluation with incomplete relevance data: a comparative study of three measures (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221614012_Retrieval_evaluation_with_incomplete_relevance_data_a_comparative_study_of_three_measures); [Estimating average precision when judgments are incomplete, Knowledge and Information Systems / Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10115-007-0101-7); [On information retrieval metrics designed for evaluation with incomplete relevance assessments, Information Retrieval Journal](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10791-008-9059-7); [On the robustness of relevance measures with incomplete judgments, SIGIR](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1277741.1277804).
- Practical remediation approach documented in the IR literature: build classifiers or distance-based extrapolation models to expand/estimate relevance judgments beyond the manually labeled pool, explicitly acknowledging that the "gold" pool is a sample, not an exhaustive set — [Document Distance for the Automated Expansion of Relevance Judgements, arXiv 1501.06380](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.06380); [LLMs Can Patch Up Missing Relevance Judgments in Evaluation, arXiv 2405.04727](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.04727); [Hybrid Pooling with LLMs via Relevance Context Learning, arXiv 2602.08457](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.08457) (the latter two suggest LLM-assisted judgment expansion is now an active, current research direction — potentially directly applicable here: an LLM could be used to independently adjudicate a sample of the highest-scoring "false positives" as a spot-check, functionally expanding the gold standard rather than just bounding uncertainty about it).
- General null-hypothesis-testing framing for reuse/copy detection explicitly recommends testing "claims of copy by chance" against a null model, structurally the same idea as testing whether top-scoring presumed-false-positives are distinguishable from true chance matches — [Estimating a Null Model of Scientific Image Reuse, arXiv 2003.00878](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.00878).

### Inferences
- A concrete, implementable sensitivity protocol for this pipeline: (1) rank all presumed-negative paragraphs by the combined/logistic-regression score; (2) for k = 0, 5, 10, 20, ... top-scoring presumed-negatives, re-run the evaluation (precision@k, FDR estimate, LOO-CV metric) once *excluding* those k items entirely (treating them as unknown/unlabeled rather than confirmed-negative) and once treating them as confirmed-positive (the opposite extreme); (3) report the resulting metric as a range/band bounded by these two extremes rather than a point estimate, explicitly stating that the true value lies somewhere in this band given the commentary's non-exhaustiveness. This is a direct, low-cost adaptation of the IR "judgment completeness sensitivity" literature and requires no new modeling, only re-running existing evaluation code with a modified negative pool — a good match to the stated constraint of Python-only, no new infrastructure.
- Because an LLM is already part of the toolchain context (per the surrounding project), the LLM-assisted relevance-judgment-expansion literature suggests a cheap follow-up validation step: have an LLM (or the human scholar) directly adjudicate the top ~20–50 highest-scoring presumed-negative pairs as a small independent audit sample, which would convert some of the "presumed-negative" uncertainty into either confirmed additional positives (expanding the tiny 46-passage gold standard) or confirmed negatives (validating the presumption) — this is a recommendation, not something reported as an established practice by a citation, so should be flagged as an actionable suggestion rather than a proven technique.

### Gaps
- No source specifically prescribes the exact "sweep k top-scoring items between excluded/confirmed-negative/confirmed-positive" protocol as a named, established technique — this is an inference/synthesis from the general "incomplete judgments materially bias metrics" literature rather than a single directly-citable named method. The report should present it as recommended practice synthesized from the IR literature, not as an established named protocol.

## Key Question 5: Multiple-comparisons correction (FDR) when tests number in the hundreds of thousands and are correlated/non-independent

### Takeaway
Standard Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) FDR control assumes independence or a specific positive-dependence structure (PRDS) among p-values and can be anti-conservative when that assumption is violated by strongly correlated, overlapping candidate pairs (many pairs sharing a target passage or source verse); the correct, well-established fallback for arbitrary/unknown dependence is the more conservative Benjamini-Yekutieli (BY) procedure, though it is a known practical trade-off (much lower power) rather than a free fix, and clustering/grouping tests by shared target or shared source verse before correction is the standard way to reduce the effective multiplicity and dependence problem.

### Cited Findings
- The original BH procedure was proven valid (controls FDR at level α) only under independence or PRDS (positive regression dependency on subset) of the test statistics — [The Control of the False Discovery Rate in Multiple Testing Under Dependency, Benjamini & Yekutieli (Semantic Scholar/ResearchGate)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/THE-CONTROL-OF-THE-FALSE-DISCOVERY-RATE-IN-MULTIPLE-Benjamini-Yekutieli/156e7730b8ba8a08ec97eb6c2eaaf2124ed0ce6e).
- Benjamini & Yekutieli's (2001) key result: applying the BH procedure at a corrected threshold α/(Σ_{j=1}^{p} 1/j) (i.e., dividing by the p-th harmonic number instead of by p) controls the FDR under arbitrary, unspecified dependence structure among the p-values; this correction is known and explicitly documented to be "often too conservative in practice" for large p (the harmonic-number correction grows like ln(p), which for hundreds of thousands of tests is a substantial power loss compared to plain BH) — [same Benjamini-Yekutieli source]; further discussion of the c(m) adjustment function under different dependence assumptions and resampling-based alternatives requiring only "subset pivotality" — [Resampling-based false discovery rate controlling multiple test procedures for correlated test statistics, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378375899000415).
- Practical middle ground documented in the literature: when the tests satisfy PRDS specifically (a weaker, directional form of positive dependence — plausible, though not guaranteed, for scores that share an underlying corpus and scoring function), plain uncorrected BH remains valid without the conservative BY penalty — meaning the choice between plain BH and BY should be justified by checking whether the correlated pairs are likely to exhibit this specific positive-dependence structure, not simply defaulting to either extreme — [same Benjamini-Yekutieli source]; alternative modifications for arbitrary correlation in high-dimensional genomic-style data (an analogous many-tests/many-correlated-tests setting) are discussed in — [Modifying the false discovery rate procedure based on information theory under arbitrary correlation, BMC Bioinformatics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12859-024-05678-w); and a symmetrized-data-aggregation approach for general dependence — [False Discovery Rate Control Under General Dependence By Symmetrized Data Aggregation, arXiv 2002.11992](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.11992).
- The Karlin-Altschul/BLAST E-value framework (see Key Question 1) is itself a built-in multiplicity correction: an E-value already represents "expected number of comparably-or-better-scoring hits under the null, given this many comparisons," which is conceptually closer to a per-test Bonferroni-style correction computed analytically rather than empirically — worth noting as an alternative route to reporting corrected significance that sidesteps the correlated-p-value problem for the n-gram/edge-overlap signals specifically, since it corrects for multiplicity as part of deriving the score's null distribution rather than as a post-hoc step over a list of p-values.

### Inferences
- Standard FDR theory does *not* apply cleanly here without adaptation: with hundreds of thousands of candidate pairs where many pairs share a target paragraph (so their scores are highly correlated — a good match will inflate the scores of several overlapping n-gram windows within the same paragraph) or share a source verse (a frequently-quoted verse like a famous Psalm will score high against many paragraphs simultaneously, again inducing correlation), the independence/PRDS assumption behind plain BH is questionable, but so is treating the correlation as fully "arbitrary" (which would force the very conservative BY correction and could suppress genuine discoveries in an already tiny expected-true-positive setting).
- A defensible middle path for this pipeline: (a) **aggregate first, test second** — reduce the multiplicity problem by testing at the level of (target paragraph, source verse) *pairs after taking the max or top score per pair* rather than testing every raw n-gram/edge match individually, which collapses much of the within-pair correlation before FDR correction is even applied; (b) explicitly note in the report that with a target-passage or source-verse clustering structure, a natural approach is to apply BH *within* correlated clusters (e.res., per source-verse group) and then correct across clusters using BY or a Bonferroni-style correction across the (much smaller) number of clusters, rather than running one flat correction across all raw pairs; (c) given the discovery set is expected to be very small (tens of true positives against hundreds of thousands of tests), any FDR-controlled discovery list at conventional q=0.05–0.10 may end up nearly empty, which is itself an honest and reportable finding rather than a failure — the report should state the corrected discovery count explicitly rather than only reporting uncorrected top-k lists.

### Gaps
- Did not find a source specifically deriving FDR correction theory for "many overlapping/nested candidate pairs sharing anchors" (the specific structure of this pipeline, where pairs share either a target-side or source-side anchor) — genomics/GWAS literature on correlated SNP tests and the general BY/PRDS literature are the closest analogues found, but a tailored derivation for this exact bipartite-pair-correlation structure would need to be worked out by the pipeline's own statisticians rather than taken off-the-shelf from a single paper.
- Did not verify how many of the sources above are peer-reviewed vs. preprint; several 2024–2026 arXiv papers (e.g., FDR under general dependence, LLM-assisted relevance judgment expansion) are recent and not yet corroborated by independent replication — flagged here as current/active-research rather than settled consensus.

## Overall honesty assessment of the existing pipeline's practices (as requested by the objective)

- **Length-quantile z-scoring**: defensible as a *first-pass* confound control and a reasonable choice given implementation constraints, but not defensible as the final word — it is a discretized, sample-hungry special case of established "continuous norming" (quantile regression) methods, and its documented residual r≈0.07–0.12 correlation (not fully removed) is consistent with known limitations of binning under heavy-tailed backgrounds. A fixable gap, not a fatal flaw: report it as a first-pass method with explicitly quantified residual confounding, and cite quantile regression / EVT-based null models as a concrete next step, not as things already ruled out.
- **Leave-one-target-out logistic regression evaluation**: defensible as the correct *type* of procedure given <25 evaluable groups (superior to a conventional split), but the single point estimate it yields should never be reported alone — it needs a bootstrap-based interval and, ideally, a permutation test for whether the ensemble's improvement over any single signal is distinguishable from chance. Reporting the raw LOO point estimate without an uncertainty band is the one practice here closest to a real, fixable methodological gap rather than a merely-suboptimal-but-acceptable choice.
- **Presumed-negative background**: defensible as the necessary starting point given a non-exhaustive gold standard (there is no way around some form of "presumed negative" with this data), but treating a presumed-negative pool as equivalent to confirmed-negative for headline precision/FDR claims is not defensible — this is the clearest fixable gap identified: the fix is not to abandon the presumed-negative background but to (a) prefer bpref-style "judged-pairs-only" metrics as the honest headline number, (b) report PU-learning-style corrected estimates only as an explicitly-caveated sensitivity analysis, and (c) run the top-k exclusion/inclusion sensitivity sweep described in Key Question 4 so that any precision/FDR claim in the final report comes with an explicit, quantified band reflecting the gold standard's known incompleteness rather than a false-precision point estimate.
