# SOTA in Computational Detection of Literary Allusion, Quotation, and Text Reuse (2024–2026), Beyond Lexical N-gram / Sentence-Embedding / Dependency-Overlap Baselines

## Retrieval-augmented, cross-encoder re-ranking, and hybrid dense+sparse architectures for text reuse / allusion detection

### Takeaway
Two-stage "cheap retrieval → expensive re-ranking" architectures (BM25/dense retrieval followed by cross-encoder or LLM-based re-scoring) are the dominant modern IR pattern and consistently beat single-signal retrieval by wide margins in general-domain benchmarks, but direct, quantified applications of ColBERT-style late interaction or cross-encoders specifically to *literary allusion/text-reuse* detection are not well documented in the literature found; the most directly relevant "second-stage" innovation for this exact use case is not a cross-encoder but an **LLM-as-verifier/agentic re-ranker** that reads full candidate pairs and grounds its judgment in text spans (see the Classical Chinese histories paper below), which is a better fit for a small-gold-standard, single-fixed-corpus setting than fine-tuned cross-encoders would be.

### Cited Findings
- Cross-encoders process the full query-document pair jointly through a transformer to compute a ranking score and are the most accurate re-ranking architecture, but are computationally expensive because every candidate pair requires a full forward pass — [Cross-Encoders, ColBERT, and LLM-Based Re-Rankers: A Practical Guide](https://medium.com/@aimichael/cross-encoders-colbert-and-llm-based-re-rankers-a-practical-guide-a23570d88548)
- ColBERT's late-interaction architecture (separate encoding of query/document, token-level MaxSim comparison) achieves ranking quality close to full cross-encoders while being markedly cheaper, and in one reported comparison was 2.2x faster than a cross-encoder with 92% top-5 result overlap — [Reranking with ColBERT: Precision Without Pain](https://medium.com/@ThinkingLoop/reranking-with-colbert-precision-without-pain-b390dda517f0)
- Hybrid sparse+dense retrieval (BM25 combined with dense embeddings via Reciprocal Rank Fusion or convex score combination) consistently improves recall by 15–30% over either signal alone in general retrieval benchmarks, and is described as "the minimum viable baseline for any RAG deployment" — [From BM25 to Corrective RAG: Benchmarking Retrieval Strategies](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01733)
- In a financial-document retrieval benchmark, **BM25 outperformed a state-of-the-art dense retriever outright** on domain-specific documents with precise terminology, and a hybrid+neural-reranking pipeline reached Recall@5 = 0.816, beating all single-stage methods — [From BM25 to Corrective RAG](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01733)
- The most concrete example of a re-ranking-like architecture applied directly to intertextuality/allusion detection is **"Beyond Similarity": Grounded Agentic Extraction** for Classical Chinese histories: rather than a cross-encoder, the authors have an LLM act as an agent that reads two full candidate text units, is forced through a constrained tool interface to ground each proposed reuse claim in exact character spans, and classifies it against a five-dimension reuse typology; tested across twelve different LLMs, precision ranged 56%–93% with a 51× cost spread between models of comparable quality; scaled to the full Twenty-Four Histories corpus (65,380 pairwise comparisons), it surfaced 5,766 confirmed intertextual pairs and found that citations became progressively *less literal* (more paraphrastic) over the 18-century span of the corpus — [arXiv:2607.27595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27595)
- PAN 2025's new "Generative Plagiarism Detection" shared task explicitly frames modern text-reuse detection as two sub-problems — **source retrieval** and **text alignment** — mirroring the retrieve-then-align pattern, evaluated on a large LLM-paraphrase-generated dataset (Llama, DeepSeek-R1, Mistral) rather than literary/historical allusion — [Overview of the Plagiarism Detection Task at PAN 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06805)

### Inferences
- For this project's setting (31,102 fixed Bible verses as the "corpus," one novel as the "query" document, ~65 gold labels), a full ColBERT or trained cross-encoder is almost certainly overkill and undertrained given the label budget — but the *architectural pattern* (cheap high-recall stage 1, expensive high-precision stage 2) is exactly the shape of the existing pipeline's problem: the dependency-edge method already gives 33% recall (the best of the three baselines) but "very poor precision," which is the classic symptom of a retrieval stage that needs a *re-ranking* stage on top of it rather than a better single signal.
- The Classical Chinese paper's design — LLM reads the *full candidate passage pair* (not just an embedding), is forced to point at exact spans, and is checked against an explicit typology of reuse types (not a binary yes/no) — is a directly transferable recipe: run the existing three-signal pipeline (or just the dependency-overlap method) as a high-recall candidate generator, then have the locally-hosted Qwen2.5-7B model act as a verifier/re-ranker over the candidate pairs it surfaces, forcing it to cite specific matching phrases and to label the *type* of reuse (verbatim / near-verbatim / thematic paraphrase / structural echo) rather than a single similarity score. This requires no training data beyond a handful of few-shot exemplars and is feasible on a single 12GB GPU because it is inference-only, not fine-tuning, and the candidate set per novel-passage is small (top-k from stage 1, not all 31,102 verses).
- The finding that BM25 can beat dense retrieval on domain-specific, precise-terminology text is a data point that generalizes the project's own finding that lexical n-gram methods have the best precision characteristics of the three baselines, even though embeddings should in principle capture "meaning."

### Gaps
- No published work was found that runs a trained ColBERT or cross-encoder model specifically on a Bible-vs-literary-novel allusion task with reported P/R/F1, so its relative benefit over the existing dependency-edge + logistic regression combination for *this exact task* cannot be directly quantified from the literature; this would need to be tested empirically rather than assumed from general IR benchmarks.
- No information was found on inference latency/cost for running a 7B-parameter LLM as a verifier over enough candidate pairs to cover a full 19th-century novel against 31,102 verses; the Chinese-histories paper's 65,380-comparison figure is a scale reference but ran on a mix of (unspecified, likely larger/API) LLMs, not confirmed on a single 12GB-VRAM local machine.

## Shared tasks / benchmarks on allusion, biblical/classical intertextuality, or quotation detection (2023–2026) with quantitative results

### Takeaway
There is no PAN/CLEF/LT4HALA/LaTeCH-CLfL shared task found that specifically targets *allusion or biblical/classical intertextuality detection* with a public leaderboard and comparable P/R/F1 numbers; the closest things are (a) individual (non-shared-task) papers proposing new datasets/models for biblical, classical, and Chinese-historical intertextuality, and (b) PAN's plagiarism/text-alignment tasks, which are adjacent but focus on modern LLM-generated plagiarism rather than allusive reuse of an ancient/scriptural source.

### Cited Findings
- LaTeCH-CLfL 2024 (8th Joint SIGHUM Workshop, EACL 2024) proceedings include quotation-related work such as "Distinguishing Fictional Voices: a Study of Authorship Verification Models for Quotation Attribution," but this addresses *speaker attribution* of already-identified quotations in fiction, not detection of biblical/classical allusion — [LaTeCH-CLfL 2024 proceedings](https://aclanthology.org/volumes/2024.latechclfl-1/); [quote_AV code](https://github.com/deezer/quote_AV)
- LT4HALA 2024 (LREC-COLING) ran shared tasks EvaLatin (Latin dependency parsing) and EvaHan (Ancient Chinese sentence segmentation/punctuation) — neither is an allusion- or quotation-detection task — [LT4HALA 2024 proceedings](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lt4hala-1.0/)
- PAN 2025 ran four shared tasks: generative AI detection, multilingual text detoxification, multi-author writing style analysis, and (new) generative plagiarism detection (source retrieval + text alignment against LLM-paraphrased scientific text) — none targets literary/scriptural allusion specifically — [Overview of PAN 2025](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-04354-2_21)
- Outside shared tasks, several 2024–2026 single-paper efforts propose new intertextuality benchmarks: MiqraBERT for Biblical Hebrew parallel detection (regression-tuned Sentence-BERT) — [arXiv:2606.19638](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19638); an "Intertextual Parallel Detection in Biblical Hebrew" paper comparing lexical, embedding, hybrid, and LLM-fine-tuned methods in an explicitly low-resource setting, concluding transformer-based approaches outperform lexical baselines overall, but that paraphrastic-parallel detection remains harder than verbatim-parallel detection — [arXiv:2506.24117](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.24117v2); a 2,533-pair expert-adjudicated benchmark for Classical Chinese intertextuality (see above) — [arXiv:2607.27595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27595); and "aSimMatrix," a scalable framework for benchmarking intertextual similarity generally — [Journal of Open Humanities Data](https://openhumanitiesdata.metajnl.com/articles/10.5334/johd.486)
- A rules-based (non-neural) system for detecting Psalmic text reuse in the New Testament uses only traditional NLP — tokenization, lemmatization, POS tagging, stop-word filtering, and WordNet-style synset assignment — as its similarity signal, i.e. essentially the same lexical/semantic-overlap family already tried in the pilot, published as recently as 2024/2025 — [Detection and Typology of Psalmic Text Reuses in the New Testament](https://doi.org/10.3390/rel17010088)
- A 2026 paper on Aggadic (rabbinic) literature also reports "complex quotation pattern" detection using classic NLP feature pipelines (tokenization/lemmatization/POS/synsets) rather than modern retrieval-reranking architectures — [Automatic detection of complex quotation patterns in Aggadic literature](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23311983.2026.2657191)

### Inferences
- The absence of a mature shared task specifically on scriptural/classical allusion detection (unlike, say, authorship verification at PAN, which has years of leaderboard history) suggests this remains a niche, low-maturity problem area computationally — which is consistent with the pilot's own observation that a straightforward LR-combination of standard signals only modestly improves results: there is no large body of tuned, benchmarked methods to draw on, and most published biblical/classical intertextuality work (Psalmic reuse, Aggadic quotations) is *still using* the same lexical/lemma/POS/synset-overlap family as the pilot's baseline #1, not a demonstrably superior alternative.
- The field appears to be actively moving toward two alternative frontiers instead of a shared task: (1) fine-tuned small sentence-embedding models for a specific scriptural corpus (MiqraBERT-style), and (2) LLM-agent-based extraction with typology and span-grounding (Classical Chinese paper) — both are more promising leads for this project than waiting for a shared-task-caliber lexical/dependency method to appear.

### Gaps
- No ACH (Association for Computers and the Humanities) 2024–2026 conference proceedings were directly retrieved/verified in this research pass; ACH does not appear to publish a formal proceedings volume comparable to ACL Anthology workshops, so it is a gap that could not be closed with the search tools used.
- No CLEF-specific (as opposed to PAN, which co-locates with CLEF) allusion/quotation task was found.
- Exact P/R/F1 numbers for the Biblical Hebrew "Intertextual Parallel Detection" paper's method comparison could not be extracted (the PDF fetch returned only a qualitative summary, not the numeric results table); this is a specific number worth chasing down manually if precise SOTA benchmarks are needed for a citation.

## Contrastive/regression fine-tuning of small embedding models in low-label settings, and synthetic contrastive pair generation

### Takeaway
Yes — task-specific fine-tuning of a small sentence-embedding model is documented to help in exactly this kind of low-label, single-domain setting, and there are two concrete, low-cost recipes for generating synthetic training pairs when only a few dozen genuine positives exist: (1) LLM-based translation/paraphrase of the source-language passages to manufacture positive pairs, and (2) using known citation/parallel pairs from adjacent or larger corpora (other biblical parallels, other authors' known quotations) as auxiliary/transfer training signal before or alongside the small in-domain gold set.

### Cited Findings
- MiqraBERT fine-tunes a Sentence-BERT model for Biblical Hebrew *inter-verse* parallel detection using a **regression objective** (predicting a continuous similarity/parallel-strength score) rather than a standard contrastive (triplet/InfoNCE) loss, explicitly targeting the detection of *non-verbatim* parallels — i.e. paraphrastic reuse, the same core difficulty as this project — and reports this out-performs baseline (non-fine-tuned) sentence embeddings, in what the paper frames as a low-resource, specialized-domain scenario — [arXiv:2606.19638](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19638)
- SPhilBERTa, a trilingual Sentence-RoBERTa model for detecting allusions across Ancient Greek, Latin, and English, was trained in part using **automatically translated synthetic data** — English texts machine-translated into Ancient Greek to manufacture parallel/positive training pairs across languages — rather than relying solely on genuine attested allusions, and the resulting model is reported to "excel at cross-lingual semantic comprehension" for allusion detection — [arXiv:2308.12008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12008)
- General contrastive-fine-tuning literature (not literary-specific) documents that LLM-generated synthetic positive pairs are a standard low-resource data-augmentation technique: prompting an LLM to generate positive text pairs, or back-translation, or paraphrase-substitution filtered by an LM-perplexity fluency check, are all used to manufacture training pairs when genuine labeled pairs are scarce — [Resource-Efficient Adaptation of LLMs for Text Embeddings](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.22729); [Paraphrase-based Contrastive Learning for Sentence Pair Modeling](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/paraphrase-based-contrastive-learning-for-sentence-pair-modeling/wrk_952f551bc8dad67509e0a6d4f29ca735)
- The same literature also cautions that contrastive learning is **sensitive to noise** in the synthetic pairs, and that LLM-generated training data can suffer hallucination or fail to follow the generation instructions precisely, meaning synthetic pairs typically need a filtering/quality-control step (e.g., perplexity filtering, or human/LLM-judge review of a sample) rather than being used unfiltered — [Using Contrastive Fine-tuning for improving smaller LMs text embeddings](https://medium.com/@techsachin/using-contrastive-fine-tuning-for-improving-smaller-lms-text-embeddings-cf5299983ed8)
- Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA adapters on top of a frozen base embedding model, trained with a contrastive objective) is documented as a way to adapt a language model to produce better task-specific sentence embeddings without full fine-tuning — relevant given the 12GB VRAM constraint — [arXiv:2507.22729](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.22729)

### Inferences
- Applied to this project: the ~65 gold biblical citations/allusions are too few to fine-tune multilingual-e5-base directly with a standard contrastive loss, but a workable recipe consistent with the literature above would be: (1) use the Ollama-hosted Qwen2.5-7B to *paraphrase* each of the 31,102 Synodal Bible verses several times in a Church-Slavonic-inflected or free-paraphrase register (the model's own generation, filtered for fluency/faithfulness), creating synthetic positive (verse, paraphrase) pairs at scale; (2) optionally supplement with genuine positive pairs mined from *other* Russian texts with well-known, previously catalogued biblical allusions (if any such catalogued corpus exists) as auxiliary signal, exactly as the key questions suggest; (3) LoRA-fine-tune multilingual-e5-base (or a comparable small model) with a contrastive or regression objective on this combined synthetic+auxiliary set, keeping the ~65 gold pairs entirely held out for evaluation. This is feasible on a single RTX 3060 (12GB) because LoRA fine-tuning of a "base"-sized (≈300M-parameter) embedding model is well within that budget, and generation of paraphrases via a local 7B model through Ollama is inference-only and slow but tractable offline.
- The regression-over-contrastive choice in MiqraBERT is worth flagging specifically: since the project's actual downstream need is a *ranked, continuous* allusion score (not a binary match/no-match), a regression objective against a graded similarity target (e.g., LLM-judged degree of paraphrase, 0–1) may be a better fit than a standard triplet contrastive loss, and is a documented, working choice for this same category of problem (verse-parallel detection in a religious source text).

### Gaps
- No numeric ablation was found isolating how much fine-tuning improved P/R/F1 in MiqraBERT specifically (over baseline off-the-shelf Sentence-BERT), so the *magnitude* of expected improvement for this project's dataset cannot be quantified from the literature; it would need to be established empirically.
- No published work was found on using **known citations from other/unrelated texts and authors as auxiliary contrastive training signal** for the specific religious/allusion detection setting — this idea appears in the general low-resource contrastive-learning literature as "in-domain plus adjacent-domain" training but was not found tested for biblical allusion detection specifically; this is a promising but empirically unverified direction for this project rather than an established, evidenced technique.

## Church Slavonic / Old Russian / archaic register normalization for downstream NLP

### Takeaway
There is active, recent (2024–2026) tooling and corpus work specifically on Old Church Slavonic morphology/lemmatization that is more principled than a hand-built ~75-entry dictionary, but the reported accuracy ceiling is still modest (roughly 38–51% depending on approach), and — critically — none of it targets the actual bottleneck the pilot identified, which is *free paraphrase*, not just archaic-vocabulary lemmatization; better morphological normalization would likely help marginally but is unlikely to be the dominant lever.

### Cited Findings
- A hybrid Old Church Slavonic lemmatizer combines rule-based handling for fragmentary tokens/punctuation/digits, a dictionary lookup for common tokens, and a sequence-to-sequence neural network with attention for the remainder, explicitly designed to handle a low-resource, morphologically complex historical language — [A Hybrid Lemmatiser For Old Church Slavonic (Afanasev)](https://wp.hse.ru/data/2021/02/18/1393879077/106LNG2021.pdf)
- A 2026 benchmarking paper ("Towards Benchmarking Old Church Slavonic Lemmatization") evaluated off-the-shelf Stanza and UDPipe-2 models (trained on the UD 2.12 treebank) against a simple dictionary baseline: the **pretrained transformer-based taggers performed poorly (≈15–16% accuracy)**, actually *below* a plain dictionary baseline (≈38%), and only retraining the models on new in-domain data pushed performance up to ≈51% — [ACL Anthology 2026.brigap-1.9](https://aclanthology.org/2026.brigap-1.9/)
- A separate dictionary-based OCS lemmatization approach is explicitly designed to avoid requiring prior linguistic knowledge of the language, making it easier to bootstrap for a specific target text — [Automatic Lemmatization of OCS Using a Novel Dictionary-Based Approach](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1007/978-3-031-70442-0_25)
- DIACU ("DIAchronic analysis of Church Slavonic") is a 2025 corpus-consolidation resource that aggregates several existing Church Slavonic corpora into one collection, intended to support diachronic/historical NLP work on the language — [A dataset for the DIAchronic analysis of Church Slavonic, BSNLP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/pdf/bsnlp/2025.bsnlp-1.12.pdf)

### Inferences
- Given that off-the-shelf modern transformer taggers *underperform* a simple dictionary on Old Church Slavonic (15–16% vs. 38%), swapping the hand-built 75-entry dictionary for one of these more systematic dictionary-based or hybrid lemmatizers could plausibly raise lemma-normalization accuracy meaningfully (dictionary approaches reach ~38%, hybrid/retrained approaches ~51%) — but this is a ceiling well below what would be needed to reliably feed a lemma-overlap signal, and it is Old Church Slavonic (liturgical Church Slavonic), not the specific Church-Slavonic-*inflected* register of 19th-century literary Russian paraphrase the novel actually uses, so transferability to the exact target register is unverified.
- This strongly reinforces — rather than contradicts — the pilot's own diagnosis: even a much better archaic-lemma normalizer addresses only the vocabulary/morphology layer, while the identified bottleneck (the ~75-entry dictionary "did not meaningfully improve recall") is free paraphrase at the *semantic/structural* level, which no lemmatizer, however good, can fix. Effort is better spent on the paraphrase-detection methods in the sections above (contrastive fine-tuning, LLM verification) than on further lemma normalization.

### Gaps
- No resource was found specifically for the register actually used by the target novel — 19th-century literary Russian with Church-Slavonic-inflected biblical paraphrase — as distinct from either pure Old Church Slavonic liturgical texts or modern standard Russian; this specific register gap (neither fully OCS nor fully modern Russian) does not appear to be covered by any tool or corpus found in this research pass, and is worth flagging explicitly as unsolved.
- No transformer model pretrained specifically on historical/Church-Slavonic-register Russian (as opposed to Old Church Slavonic proper) was found.

## Topic models, discourse structure, and narrative-position features in allusion detection

### Takeaway
No evidence was found of topic modeling, discourse-structure features, or narrative-position features being used as a signal in state-of-the-art allusion/quotation/text-reuse detection systems; these techniques are well established in computational literary studies generally (theme detection, narrative discourse modeling) but appear to be a separate research thread that has not yet been integrated into allusion-detection pipelines in the literature surveyed.

### Cited Findings
- Topic modeling (LDA, LSI, NMF, HDP) is a mature, widely used technique for detecting and visualizing themes across literary corpora, and is described as "sensitive to text structure," but the surveyed material discusses this purely for thematic analysis, not for detecting reuse/allusion of a specific source text — [Applying Topic Modeling to Literary Analysis: A Review](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/dsll-2024-0010/html?lang=en)
- Narrative discourse modeling work (e.g., Elson's "Modeling Narrative Discourse") proposes discourse relations specific to narrative (agentive characters, goals, plans, beliefs, time) for structural analysis of fiction, again as a general literary-analysis tool rather than an allusion-detection feature — [Modeling Narrative Discourse (Elson)](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~delson/pubs/Modeling-Narrative-Discourse_Elson_R4.pdf)
- Work on modeling narrative structure in Latin epic poetry via automatically generated "story grammars" (2025) is adjacent — it operates on the same kind of classical-literature corpus as allusion-detection work — but targets structural/plot modeling, not source-text reuse detection — [Modeling Narrative Structure in Latin Epic Poetry (arXiv:2502.12276)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12276)

### Inferences
- The complete absence of topic-model or narrative-position features in the allusion-detection literature surveyed suggests this is a genuinely open/unexplored direction rather than a tried-and-rejected one — it cannot be recommended as an established "SOTA" technique, but it is also not contraindicated. One plausible, low-cost feature to test given the tools already in this project would be a coarse discourse/narrative-position feature (e.g., is the candidate passage in narration vs. dialogue; proximity to other already-confirmed allusions in the text) as an auxiliary feature in the existing logistic-regression combiner, since these are cheap to compute and the LR stage already exists — but this is this researcher's inference, not a documented finding, and should be labeled speculative if used.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that tests topic-model or discourse/narrative-position features specifically as *predictors* of allusion/citation likelihood (as opposed to independent literary-analysis outputs). This entire key question is essentially an open gap in the current published literature as far as this search could determine.

## Embeddings vs. lexical/n-gram methods for ALLUSIVE (non-verbatim) text reuse — recent comparative findings

### Takeaway
Recent literature broadly agrees with part of what the pilot has already observed — n-gram/lexical methods excel at verbatim/near-verbatim reuse but degrade sharply on paraphrastic/allusive reuse, and contextual embeddings are explicitly positioned as the remedy for exactly that gap — but published comparative work does *not* generally report embeddings "underperforming" the way the pilot's multilingual-e5-base did (5% recall, poor ranking); instead it reports embeddings as an improvement over pure lexical matching for allusive reuse, which is a point of tension with this project's own empirical result and worth flagging as a discrepancy rather than resolving it in the model's favor.

### Cited Findings
- "N-gram and alignment approaches are effective for verbatim or near-verbatim borrowing but struggle with allusive reuse and paraphrastic borrowing, whereas contextual language models offer efficient semantic representations to detect not only near-verbatim reuses but also paraphrases and allusions" — summary drawn from search results surveying the "Modelling Intertextuality with N-gram Embeddings" paper and related work — [arXiv:2509.06637](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06637)
- The "Modelling Intertextuality with N-gram Embeddings" paper itself proposes a *hybrid* of the two families rather than picking a winner: it performs pairwise comparison of **n-gram-level embeddings** (not whole-sentence embeddings, and not raw lexical n-grams) between two texts and averages the results into an overall intertextuality score, validated on four texts with known degrees of intertextuality plus a 267-text scalability test with network analysis of resulting communities — [arXiv:2509.06637](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06637v1)
- The Biblical Hebrew "Intertextual Parallel Detection" comparison explicitly reports that transformer/embedding-based approaches outperform traditional lexical baselines overall, but that **paraphrastic parallels remain harder to detect than verbatim ones for every method tested**, i.e., the embedding advantage over lexical methods narrows (but does not disappear or reverse) specifically on the paraphrastic subset — [arXiv:2506.24117](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.24117v2)
- Outside the literary domain, general IR benchmarking work found BM25 (a lexical method) can outright *beat* a modern dense embedding retriever on domain-specific text with precise, repeated terminology — [From BM25 to Corrective RAG](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01733)

### Inferences
- This project's finding that multilingual-e5-base (a general-purpose, off-the-shelf multilingual sentence embedding model) gave only 5% recall with poor ranking is *not* necessarily in tension with "embeddings help with paraphrase" claims in the literature, because the papers reporting embeddings as helpful for allusive reuse are generally using either (a) n-gram-level (not whole-passage) embedding comparison — a finer granularity than the pilot's sentence-level cosine similarity — or (b) a **task/domain fine-tuned** embedding model (MiqraBERT, SPhilBERTa), not an off-the-shelf general-purpose one. This suggests the pilot's embedding result is better read as "off-the-shelf, whole-passage embeddings underperform" rather than "embeddings as a family underperform," and that the literature's more favorable view of embeddings is conditional on fine-tuning and/or finer-grained (n-gram or clause-level, not whole-sentence/paragraph-level) comparison — both directions the pilot has not yet tried.
- The dependency-edge method's profile in the pilot (best raw recall at 33%, but "very poor precision") is not directly addressed by any paper found in this search as a documented general pattern (i.e., no paper explicitly states "syntax/dependency overlap gives best recall, worst precision" as a generalizable finding) — this specific three-way tradeoff characterization appears to be original to the pilot's own experiments rather than confirmed or contradicted by the broader literature.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that runs a head-to-head comparison of *whole-passage* embeddings vs. *n-gram-level* embeddings vs. lexical n-grams vs. dependency overlap on the same religious-text allusion task, which is exactly the comparison that would validate or refute the granularity-based explanation offered above; this remains an inference, not a confirmed finding.
- No paper was found explicitly discussing dependency/syntactic-edge overlap as a family of methods for allusion detection at all (as opposed to lexical or embedding methods) — so no independent evidence could be found either supporting or refuting the pilot's specific finding about dependency overlap's precision/recall tradeoff.

## Summary of most actionable leads for this specific project

1. **LLM-as-verifier re-ranking stage** (modeled on the Classical Chinese histories "grounded agentic extraction" approach, arXiv:2607.27595): use the existing three-signal pipeline (especially the high-recall dependency-overlap method) as a candidate generator, then have the local Qwen2.5-7B model re-score/verify each candidate pair by reading the full passage pair, citing matching spans, and classifying reuse type — inference-only, feasible on 12GB VRAM, no additional labeled training data required.
2. **LoRA/regression fine-tuning of a small embedding model on synthetic pairs** (modeled on MiqraBERT's regression objective and SPhilBERTa's synthetic-translation training data): use Qwen2.5-7B to generate paraphrased/Church-Slavonic-inflected versions of Bible verses as synthetic positive pairs, fine-tune multilingual-e5-base (or similar) via LoRA with a regression or contrastive objective, holding the ~65 gold pairs out for evaluation only.
3. **Better Old Church Slavonic lemmatization tooling** (dictionary-based or hybrid lemmatizers, ~38–51% reported accuracy vs. an ad hoc 75-entry dictionary) is available and could be swapped in, but the literature and this project's own prior negative result together suggest this addresses a secondary bottleneck (archaic vocabulary), not the primary one (free paraphrase) — low priority relative to (1) and (2).
4. **Hybrid BM25+dense retrieval as a first-stage candidate generator**, feeding into whichever re-ranker is chosen, is a well-evidenced general-IR pattern (15–30% recall improvement over single-signal retrieval) though not validated specifically on allusion detection — worth testing as a cheap architectural change before or alongside (1).
5. Topic modeling / narrative-position features and dependency-edge-overlap-as-a-documented-method are both **genuine gaps** in the literature — neither confirmed nor refuted as useful for this task — so any work here would be exploratory rather than literature-backed.
